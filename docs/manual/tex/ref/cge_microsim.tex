\cgeentry{cge_microsim}{Pass simulations to household survey data}

\cgesec{Description}

\cmd{cge_microsim} passes the results of one or more simulations to the
household survey in memory, top-down and without feedback to the model. Each survey household
belongs to a household group of the model and keeps its own income
composition and, if they are given, its own budget shares. Each income
component changes as the model says that its source changes for the
household's group; nominal consumption follows the household's income and its
group's direct tax and savings rates; and consumption is deflated by a price
index at the prices households pay, including VAT. The command reports real
consumption per person, the poverty headcount and poverty gap, and the Gini
coefficient, before and after the simulation, for all households and for
each group, and can add the household's new consumption and price index to
the data. From version 0.8.0 it also gives, for several simulations at once,
poverty for several lines, including lines that differ by household, the
number of poor, eight more inequality measures, incidence by quantile of
base consumption and the growth incidence curve, and all results as a
dataset. From version 0.8.1 it gives each household's equivalent and
compensating variations, and divides every change among its channels; from
version 0.8.2, fiscal incidence as the Commitment to Equity method measures
it, before and after the simulation; from version 0.8.3, it can reweight the
survey to the model's employment, give standard errors from the survey
design, and give the range of the results across draws of the elasticities.

\cgesec{Quick start}

Pass simulation \cmd{notariff} to the survey in memory, in which \cmd{grp}
holds each household's model group and \cmd{cons} its consumption; every
household's consumption changes as its group's does
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim, sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons)
\end{stcode}
As above, with sampling weights \cmd{w}, consumption per person and a poverty
line of 1,200 per person
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) line(1200)
\end{stcode}
Households' own incomes from wages, profits, remittances and grants, and
their own budget shares for food, goods and services
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    income(wage:lab profit:cap remit:row grants:gov)                              ///
    budget(food:com_agr goods:com_man services:com_ser) line(1200)
\end{stcode}
As above, and add the variables \cmd{nt_nom}, \cmd{nt_price}, \cmd{nt_real},
\cmd{nt_pc} and \cmd{nt_q}, replacing them if they exist
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    income(wage:lab profit:cap remit:row grants:gov) line(1200) generate(nt) replace
\end{stcode}
Two simulations, a national line and regional lines in the variable
\cmd{zreg}, quintiles, and every table
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff vat18) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    income(wage:lab profit:cap remit:row grants:gov) line(1200 zreg) quantiles(5) detail
\end{stcode}
Welfare by household, and the contribution of each channel to every change
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    income(wage:lab profit:cap remit:row grants:gov) line(1200) generate(w1) decompose
\end{stcode}
Fiscal incidence of direct taxes \cmd{dtx}, transfers \cmd{grants}, indirect
taxes \cmd{itx} and health services \cmd{hlt}, which follow government
purchases of \cmd{com_hlt}
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(hfund) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    income(wage:lab profit:cap remit:row grants:gov) line(1200) market(ym)        ///
    dtax(dtx) transfers(grants) itax(itx) inkind(hlt:com_hlt)
\end{stcode}
Weights adjusted so that the households' workers in agriculture, \cmd{agri},
follow the model's employment of labour there; standard errors from the
survey design; and bands across the draws kept by \manref{cge_sensitivity}
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    line(1200) reweight(agri:lab.act_agr)
svyset psu [pweight = w], strata(region)
cge_microsim, sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) line(1200) svy
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    line(1200) bands(dr)
\end{stcode}
As above, with the results as a dataset, saved to \cmd{incidence.dta}
\begin{stcode}
cge_microsim [pweight = w], sim(notariff vat18) group(grp) consumption(cons) size(hhsize) ///
    income(wage:lab profit:cap remit:row grants:gov) line(1200 zreg) saving(incidence)
\end{stcode}

\cgesec{Options}

\begin{optdesc}
\item[\optarg{}{sim}{names}] names the simulations whose results are passed to
the survey: simulations solved by \manref{cge_solve}, periods of a run of
\manref{cge_dynamic}, or solutions imported by \mbox{\manref{cge_gams}}. Changes
are measured from the calibrated base. Several simulations may be given from
version 0.8.0; \cmd{r()} then holds the results of the first, and
\cmd{clear} or \cmd{saving()} give those of every one.

\item[\optarg{}{group}{varname}] gives each household's group: a string
variable whose values are labels of the model's households, or a numeric
variable whose value labels, or failing those whose values, are. A value that
is not a household of the model is an error.

\item[\optarg{cons}{umption}{varname}] is the household's total consumption,
the welfare measure, in the survey's units.

\item[\optarg{}{size}{varname}] is the household's size, which must be
positive. Real consumption, poverty and inequality are then measured per
person, over persons. The default is a size of 1.

\item[\optarg{inc}{ome}{var\texttt{:}source \ldots}] lists the household's
income components, numeric variables, each with its source:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\labelsep}{0.6em}
\item a factor of the model: the group's income from that factor, or the
factor's income if the group has none in the base;
\item \cmd{gov}: transfers from the government, set in real terms and paid at the consumer price index;
\item \cmd{row}: transfers from abroad, such as remittances;
\item \cmd{dom}: transfers from other domestic institutions, such as
dividends;
\item \cmd{cpi}: income indexed to the consumer price index;
\item \cmd{fixed}: nominal income that does not change.
\end{itemize}
Without \cmd{income()}, every household's nominal consumption changes as its
group's does.

\item[\optarg{bud}{get}{var\texttt{:}commodity \ldots}] lists spending on
budget items, numeric and not negative, and the commodity whose price applies
to each. An item may instead name an activity, for home consumption of its
output at the activity's price. A household's price index then uses its own
budget shares; without \cmd{budget()}, or for a household whose items sum to
zero, it uses its group's base budget shares, including home consumption.

\item[\optarg{}{line}{lines}] gives one or more poverty lines per person, in
the units of consumption: positive numbers, or (from version 0.8.0) numeric
variables that give each household its own line, such as a regional line or a
line that depends on the household's composition. A household whose line is
missing is left out. The first line is the one shown in the main table and in
\res{r(table)}. Without \cmd{line()}, no poverty measures are computed.

\item[\optarg{quant}{iles}{\#}] sets the number of groups of persons, ranked by
base consumption per person, for incidence: 10, the default, for deciles, 5
for quintiles, and so on, from 2 to 100.

\item[\optarg{gen}{erate}{prefix}] adds the variables \argu{prefix}\cmd{_nom}
(new nominal consumption), \argu{prefix}\cmd{_price} (the household's price
index, relative to the base), \argu{prefix}\cmd{_real} (real consumption, at base
prices), \argu{prefix}\cmd{_pc} (real consumption per person) and
\argu{prefix}\cmd{_ev} and \argu{prefix}\cmd{_cv} (the household's equivalent
and compensating variations, from version 0.8.1), and \argu{prefix}\cmd{_q}
(the household's quantile group of base consumption per person). With several
simulations, all but \argu{prefix}\cmd{_q} are numbered in the order of
\cmd{sim()}: \argu{prefix}\cmd{_nom1}, \argu{prefix}\cmd{_nom2}, and so on.
They are missing for observations outside the sample. \argu{prefix} may have
at most 26 characters, fewer the more simulations there are.

\item[\opt{}{replace}] allows the variables of \cmd{generate()} to be
replaced. Without it, a variable that exists is an error.

\item[\opt{det}{ail}] also displays, for each simulation, poverty for every
line and group, the inequality measures for all households, the equivalent and
compensating variations by group, and incidence by quantile.

\item[\opt{dec}{ompose}] (from version 0.8.1) divides the change in every
measure for all households among its channels, by the Shapley method: each
source of income named in \cmd{income()}; \cmd{taxsav}, the direct tax and
savings rates of the household's group; \cmd{group}, for households without
income in the components given, which follow their group's consumption; and
\cmd{prices}. Without \cmd{income()}, the channels are \cmd{income}, the
group's consumption, and \cmd{prices}. The contributions add up to the change.
The change in each poverty measure is also divided into growth and
redistribution. With $K$ channels, every measure is computed for $2^K$
distributions; $K$ may be at most 12.

\item[\optarg{mark}{et}{varname}] (from version 0.8.2) is the household's
market income, before taxes and transfers, in the units of consumption. It
ranks persons, by market income per person, for fiscal incidence, and is
required with the instruments below, which are amounts that the household
pays or receives and cannot be negative. Market income changes in the
simulation as the household's income components do, or as its group's
income without \cmd{income()}.

\item[\optarg{dt}{ax}{varlist}] lists direct taxes and contributions paid.
Each changes with market income and the group's direct tax rate.

\item[\optarg{trans}{fers}{varlist}] lists direct transfers received, which
change as the group's transfers from the government.

\item[\optarg{it}{ax}{varlist}] lists indirect taxes paid, which change with
the household's nominal spending and with the share of sales tax and VAT in
the prices it pays, at its own or its group's budget shares.

\item[\optarg{sub}{sidies}{varlist}] lists indirect subsidies received, which
change with nominal spending.

\item[\optarg{ink}{ind}{var\texttt{:}target \ldots}] lists in-kind benefits,
such as health or education services, valued at cost, each with the model's
spending it follows: a commodity, whose purchases by the government give the
change, or \cmd{shi}, the purchases of the health insurance fund.

\item[\optarg{rew}{eight}{var\texttt{:}target \ldots}] (from version 0.8.3)
adjusts the survey weights in the simulation so that the weighted total of
each household characteristic \argu{var}, a non-negative variable such as
the household's number of workers in a sector, changes as its target does: a
factor of the model, for its employment in all activities;
\argu{factor}\cmd{.}\argu{activity}, for its employment in that activity; or a
positive number, a ratio given by the user, such as the growth of the
population. The new weights are as close to the survey's as the targets
allow, in relative entropy. The simulated results use them, the base results
the survey's weights, and \cmd{decompose} has a channel \cmd{weights}.

\item[\opt{}{svy}] (from version 0.8.3) computes linearized standard errors
of mean consumption per person and of FGT0, FGT1 and FGT2 for each line, in
the base, the simulation and of the change, for all households and each
group, as a subpopulation, from the design declared by \cmd{svyset}: one
stage, with strata and clusters, without a finite population correction. The
weights are those of \cmd{svyset}, and a weight may not be given as well.
The standard errors measure the sampling error of the survey, taking the
model's changes as given.

\item[\optarg{band}{s}{name}] (from version 0.8.3) gives the range of the
results for all households across the draws of a sensitivity analysis kept
as the simulations \argu{name}\cmd{1}, \argu{name}\cmd{2}, \ldots\ by
\manref{cge_sensitivity} with \cmd{keep(}\argu{name}\cmd{)}: for each measure,
its 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles and standard deviation across the draws,
beside its value in the first simulation of \cmd{sim()}.

\item[\opt{}{clear}] replaces the survey in memory with the results, as a
dataset (see \emph{Results as a dataset} below). It cannot be combined with
\cmd{generate()}, whose variables would be lost.

\item[\optarg{sav}{ing}{filename[\texttt{, replace}]}] saves the results as a
dataset in \argu{filename} and leaves the survey in memory; \cmd{replace}
allows an existing file to be replaced.

\item[\opt{nod}{isplay}] suppresses the tables of results.

\item[\optarg{}{graph}{graphs}] (from version 0.9.6) draws graphs of the
results, one or more of \cmd{gic}, \cmd{incidence}, \cmd{poverty},
\cmd{groups}, \cmd{shapley}, \cmd{fiscal} and \cmd{bands}, or \cmd{all} for
every graph the other options allow; see \emph{Graphs} below.

\item[\optarg{}{gname}{stub}] names the graphs \argu{stub}\cmd{_}\argu{graph},
such as \cmd{microsim_gic}; the default stub is \cmd{microsim}.

\item[\optarg{}{gexport}{filename}] saves each graph as a file named after
the graph: with \cmd{gexport(fig.pdf)}, the growth incidence curve is saved
as \cmd{fig_gic.pdf}, and so on. The extension gives the format: \cmd{.pdf}
or \cmd{.eps} for a journal, \cmd{.png} (the default, 2,000 pixels wide) for
slides and documents.

\item[\optarg{glab}{els}{labels}] gives the simulations, in the order of
\cmd{sim()}, the labels shown in the legends, such as
\cmd{glabels("Tariffs removed" "Tariffs doubled")}; by default they are the
names of the simulations.

\item[\opt{}{gcolor}] draws the series in the colours of \citet{okabe2008},
which readers with colour blindness can tell apart, instead of shades of grey.

\item[\optarg{}{gopts}{options}] adds graph options to every graph, such as
\cmd{gopts(title("Tariff reform"))} or \cmd{gopts(xsize(5) ysize(3.5))}.
\end{optdesc}

\cgesec{Remarks and examples}

A model has a few household groups; poverty and inequality are measured on
household surveys. \mbox{\cmd{cge_microsim}} links the two in the top-down way
described by Bourguignon, Bussolo and Pereira da Silva (2008): the
model gives, for each group, the change in each source of income and in each
price; the survey gives each household's income composition, budget shares,
size and weight. The survey's own income and consumption data drive the
results, so a household that lives on wages gains more than its group when
wages rise relative to other income. There is no feedback from the survey to
the model. Every substantial source of income should be mapped, and the
survey and the SAM should refer to the same year where possible.

An observation is used if it satisfies \argu{if} and \argu{in} and has no
missing value in the group, consumption, weight, size or the variables of
\cmd{income()} and \cmd{budget()}.

\cgesubsec{Example 1: every household follows its group}

The synthetic SAM is calibrated as in \manref{cge_solve}, and every import
tariff is removed, with the direct tax rates of households adjusting to keep
government savings fixed:
\stlog{ms_sim}
The survey is synthetic, generated with a fixed seed: 1,000 households of one
to six persons in the model's single group, \cmd{hhd}, with weights, incomes
from wages, profits and government transfers, and spending on food, goods
and services:
\stlog{ms_survey}
\cgeneed{16\baselineskip}
Without \cmd{income()} and \cmd{budget()}, each household's nominal
consumption changes as its group's, and its price index is the group's:
\stlog[\scriptsize]{ms_group}
The table shows, for all households and for each group in the survey, mean
real consumption per person, weighted by persons, in the base and in the
simulation, and its per cent change; the poverty headcount and poverty gap,
in per cent; and the Gini coefficient of consumption per person, in per cent.
Every household of a group changes in the same proportion, so with one group
the Gini coefficient cannot change; the poverty measures change because
households near the line cross it.

\cgesubsec{Example 2: households' own incomes and budgets}

With \cmd{income()}, each household's income components change as their
sources do for its group; with \cmd{budget()}, each household has its own
price index:
\stlog[\scriptsize]{ms_income}
Real consumption per person falls by less, 0.389 against 0.431 per cent, and
the Gini coefficient now changes, from 36.32 to 36.33 per cent, because
households with different shares of wages, profits and transfers, and of
food, goods and services, are affected differently. The survey and the SAM
are synthetic: the results illustrate the command and are not findings about
any country.

\cgesubsec{Example 3: stored results and new variables}

The measures for all households are returned as scalars, and the whole table,
with the severity of poverty (FGT2), in \res{r(table)}; poverty measures and
Gini coefficients are stored as proportions, not per cent:
\stlog{ms_return}
\cmd{generate(nt)} added each household's new nominal consumption, price
index, real consumption and real consumption per person:
\stlog{ms_vars}
These variables can be analysed with any Stata command, for example to
compute other poverty or inequality measures, or measures by region.

\cgesubsec{Example 4: several simulations and several poverty lines}

A second scenario doubles the tariffs. Both scenarios are passed to the
survey at once, with the national line of 100 and a regional line of 90 or
110 per person, held in a variable:
\stlog{ms_sim2}
\stlog[\scriptsize]{ms_multi}
The table shows each simulation in turn. With \cmd{detail}, each simulation
also has poverty for every line, the inequality measures, and incidence by
quintile (here, with \cmd{quantiles(5)}): the persons of each fifth of the
base distribution of consumption per person, their mean consumption before
and after, their shares of total consumption, and their share of the total
change. At the regional lines, 25.82 per cent of persons are poor in the base,
against 26.75 per cent at the national line, and removing the tariffs raises
the headcount by 0.58 points, against 0.16. Doubling the tariffs raises mean
consumption by 0.095 per cent, and the poorest fifth receives 7.2 per cent of
the gain while holding 6.4 per cent of consumption.

\cgesubsec{Example 5: the results as a dataset}

With \cmd{clear}, the results replace the survey in memory; \cmd{saving()}
would save them and keep the survey. Each observation is one result:
\stlog{ms_data}
The growth incidence curve is in the table \cmd{gic}: the per cent change of
each percentile of consumption per person, from the base to the simulation.
It compares the distributions, not the same households; the table
\cmd{quantile} follows the same persons:
\stlog{ms_gic}

\cgesubsec{Example 6: welfare, and the channels of the changes}

\cmd{generate()} adds each household's equivalent and compensating variations,
and \cmd{decompose} divides each change among its channels:
\stlog[\scriptsize]{ms_decomp}
\stlog{ms_ev}
Removing the tariffs raises wages, which add 2.41 per cent to mean
consumption per person, and profits, which add 0.23 per cent; the direct tax
rate rises to keep government savings fixed, which takes away 3.04 per cent;
prices add 0.01 per cent, and government transfers, fixed in real terms,
nothing. The contributions add up to the change, $-0.389$ per cent, and those
to the headcount, to its rise of 0.16 points. Growth and redistribution divide
the change in the headcount in the same way: here it is all growth, since the
change in the distribution, at either mean, moves no one across the line.
The equivalent variation, the change in consumption at base prices worth as
much as the simulation, is at least the change in real consumption for every
household, since the household can substitute among goods.

\cgesubsec{Example 7: fiscal incidence}

The survey gives market income, wages and profits; direct taxes of 10 per
cent of it; indirect taxes of 5 per cent of consumption; and free schooling,
valued at cost, for some households, which follows government purchases of
services:
\stlog[\scriptsize]{ms_fiscal}
Direct taxes, 10 per cent of market income, are proportional: their Kakwani
index is 0. Transfers and schooling, of amounts unrelated to income, are worth
relatively more to poorer persons and are progressive, with Kakwani indexes
of 0.30 and 0.32; indirect taxes, 5 per cent of consumption, take a slightly
larger share of the market income of poorer persons, and their index is
$-0.002$. Each instrument's marginal contribution is the change in the Gini
coefficient of final income that it makes. The simulation changes each
instrument with the model: the direct tax rate rises to keep government
savings fixed, so direct taxes rise from 10.0 to 13.1 per cent of market
income, and fiscal impoverishment from 29.00 to 29.45 per cent of persons.

\cgesubsec{Example 8: reweighting, standard errors and bands}

Two-fifths of the synthetic households, \cmd{agri}, work in agriculture.
With the tariffs removed, the model's employment of labour in agriculture
falls by 1.3 per cent; reweighting the survey so that the agricultural
households fall in the same proportion lowers their weights by that much and
leaves the others' unchanged, the closest weights to the survey's that meet
the target:
\stlog[\scriptsize]{ms_reweight}
With the survey design declared, in clusters of ten households in four
strata, the mean and the poverty measures have standard errors, and so do
their changes. The standard error of the change in the headcount, 0.0016, is
much smaller than that of the headcount itself, 0.0137, since the same
households are compared before and after:
\stlog{ms_svy}
A sensitivity analysis with 20 draws of the elasticities, each kept as a
simulation, gives the range of the results. Here poverty and inequality
hardly depend on the elasticities, while the equivalent variation per person
ranges from $-0.81$ to $-0.63$ between the 5th and 95th percentiles:
\stlog[\scriptsize]{ms_bands}

\end{cgetable}

\cgesec{Methods and formulas}

Household $i$ belongs to group $h = h(i)$ and has income components
$y_{ik}$ with sources $k$. The model gives, for each group, the ratio of the
simulated to the base value of each source,

\begin{cgetable}{p{0.20\linewidth}p{0.42\linewidth}p{0.30\linewidth}}
\toprule
Source & Ratio $\rho_{hk}$ & If the group's base value is zero\\
\midrule\endhead
\bottomrule\endlastfoot
factor $f$ & $\V{YIF}_{hf} / \V{YIF}^0_{hf}$ & $\V{YF}_f / \V{YF}^0_f$\\
\cmd{gov} & $\V{trnsfr}_{h,gov}\,\V{CPI} / (\V{trnsfr}^0_{h,gov}\,\V{CPI}^0)$ & $\V{CPI}/\V{CPI}^0$\\
\cmd{row} & $\V{trnsfr}_{h,row}\,\V{EXR} / (\V{trnsfr}^0_{h,row}\,\V{EXR}^0)$ & $\V{EXR}/\V{EXR}^0$\\
\cmd{dom} & $\sum_d \V{shii}_{hd}\,\V{DY}_d / \sum_d \V{shii}^0_{hd}\,\V{DY}^0_d$ & $\V{CPI}/\V{CPI}^0$\\
\cmd{cpi} & $\V{CPI}/\V{CPI}^0$ & \\
\cmd{fixed} & 1 & \\
\end{cgetable}
where $\V{DY}_d = (1 - \V{MPS}_d)(1 - \V{TINS}_d)\,\V{YI}_d$ is the income
that institution $d$ passes on or spends. The household's nominal
consumption becomes
\begin{equation*}
C^1_i = C^0_i\,\frac{\sum_k \rho_{hk}\,y_{ik}}{\sum_k y_{ik}}\,\kappa_h, \qquad
\kappa_h = \frac{(1 - \V{MPS}_h)(1 - \V{TINS}_h)}{(1 - \V{MPS}^0_h)(1 - \V{TINS}^0_h)},
\end{equation*}
and $C^1_i = C^0_i\,\V{EH}_h / \V{EH}^0_h$ for a household whose components
sum to zero, and for every household without \cmd{income()}. The household's
price index is
\begin{equation*}
P_i = \sum_j s_{ij}\,\frac{p_j}{p^0_j},
\end{equation*}
over commodities and home-consumed activity outputs $j$, where $p_j$ is the
price households pay for commodity $j$, $\V{PH}_j$, including VAT, or the
activity price $\V{PA}_j$ for home consumption; $s_{ij}$ are the household's
budget shares from \cmd{budget()} or else the group's base shares,
$p^0_j\,\V{QH}^0_{jh}/\V{EH}^0_h$ (with $\V{QHA}^0_{jh}$ for home
consumption). Real consumption is $R_i = C^1_i/P_i$, a first-order
(Laspeyres) approximation for households with LES demand, and with household
size $n_i$ and weight $w_i$, consumption per person is $r^0_i = C^0_i/n_i$ in
the base and $r^1_i = R_i/n_i$ in the simulation.

Over the households of a row of the table, with person weights
$v_i = w_i n_i$, the mean is $\sum_i v_i r_i / \sum_i v_i$, and for the
poverty line $z$
\begin{equation*}
\text{FGT}_\alpha = \frac{\sum_i v_i\,\mathbf{1}[r_i < z]\,\bigl((z - r_i)/z\bigr)^\alpha}{\sum_i v_i},
\qquad \alpha = 0, 1, 2 .
\end{equation*}
The Gini coefficient is computed from the Lorenz curve over persons: with
households sorted by $r_i$, $F_i$ the cumulative share of person weights and
$L_i$ the cumulative share of $v_i r_i$, and $F_0 = L_0 = 0$,
\begin{equation*}
G = 1 - \sum_i (F_i - F_{i-1})(L_i + L_{i-1}).
\end{equation*}
A poverty line given as a variable replaces $z$ by the household's line $z_i$.
The number of poor is $\sum_i v_i\,\mathbf{1}[r_i < z_i]$.

\textbf{Other inequality measures (from version 0.8.0).} With
$\mu = \sum_i v_i r_i / \sum_i v_i$ and $s_i = r_i / \mu$, over the persons with
$v_i > 0$,
\begin{gather*}
\text{GE}(0) = -\frac{\sum_i v_i \ln s_i}{\sum_i v_i}, \qquad
\text{GE}(1) = \frac{\sum_i v_i\, s_i \ln s_i}{\sum_i v_i}, \qquad
\text{GE}(2) = \frac{\sum_i v_i (s_i^2 - 1)}{2 \sum_i v_i},\\
A(\varepsilon) = 1 - \frac{1}{\mu}\left(\frac{\sum_i v_i\, r_i^{1-\varepsilon}}{\sum_i v_i}\right)^{1/(1-\varepsilon)},
\qquad A(1) = 1 - \frac{1}{\mu}\exp\left(\frac{\sum_i v_i \ln r_i}{\sum_i v_i}\right),
\end{gather*}
the mean log deviation, the Theil index, half the squared coefficient of
variation, and the Atkinson index for $\varepsilon = 0.5$, 1 and 2. The
measures that need $\ln r_i$, or $r_i^{1-\varepsilon}$ with $\varepsilon > 1$,
are missing when some person's consumption is 0 or less. With $L(p)$ the
Lorenz curve, linear between the points $(F_i, L_i)$, so that a household at a
boundary is shared between the groups on either side,
\begin{equation*}
\text{Palma} = \frac{1 - L(0.9)}{L(0.4)}, \qquad \text{S80/S20} = \frac{1 - L(0.8)}{L(0.2)} .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Quantiles and incidence.} With the values $r_{(1)} \le \dots \le r_{(n)}$,
$W(i)$ the cumulative person weight and $P = W(n)\,p/100$, the $p$th
percentile is $r_{(i)}$ for the first $i$ with $W(i) > P$, or
$(r_{(i-1)} + r_{(i)})/2$ when $W(i-1) = P$: the definition of \cmd{_pctile}
with weights. Persons fall into quantile group $k$ of $K$ when their base
consumption per person is above percentile $100(k-1)/K$ and up to percentile
$100k/K$, as \cmd{xtile} forms groups; tied values stay together, so groups
may hold somewhat more or less than $1/K$ of persons. For group $k$, with
$S^t_k = \sum_{i \in k} v_i r^t_i$, the table gives the population share
$\sum_{i \in k} v_i / \sum_i v_i$, mean consumption $S^t_k / \sum_{i \in k} v_i$
in the base ($t = 0$) and the simulation ($t = 1$), the shares of total
consumption $S^t_k / \sum_j S^t_j$, and the share of the total change,
$(S^1_k - S^0_k) / \sum_j (S^1_j - S^0_j)$. The growth incidence curve
\citep{ravallion2003} is $g(p) = 100\,\bigl(r^1(p)/r^0(p) - 1\bigr)$ at
$p = 1, \dots, 99$, with $r^t(p)$ the $p$th percentile of consumption per person
in the base and the simulation.

\textbf{Welfare (from version 0.8.1).} With $p_j$ the prices households
pay, $\gamma_{jh}$ and $\beta_{jh}$ the subsistence quantities and marginal
budget shares of group $h$, and $\lambda_i = C^0_i/\V{EH}^0_h$,
\begin{gather*}
EV_i = \lambda_i \sum_j p^0_j\gamma_{jh} + \Bigl(C^1_i - \lambda_i \sum_j p^1_j\gamma_{jh}\Bigr)\prod_j \Bigl(\frac{p^0_j}{p^1_j}\Bigr)^{\beta_{jh}} - C^0_i,\\
CV_i = C^1_i - \lambda_i \sum_j p^1_j\gamma_{jh} - \Bigl(C^0_i - \lambda_i \sum_j p^0_j\gamma_{jh}\Bigr)\prod_j \Bigl(\frac{p^1_j}{p^0_j}\Bigr)^{\beta_{jh}},
\end{gather*}
the measures of the group's expenditure function with subsistence quantities
$\lambda_i \gamma_{jh}$; a household with its group's change in consumption
has $\lambda_i$ times its group's $EV_h$ and $CV_h$. With \cmd{budget()}, a
household with budget items has Cobb--Douglas preferences in its own shares
$s_{ik}$: with $G_i = \prod_k (p^1_{j(k)}/p^0_{j(k)})^{s_{ik}}$,
$EV_i = C^1_i/G_i - C^0_i$ and $CV_i = C^1_i - C^0_i\,G_i$. Since $G_i \le P_i$,
$EV_i \ge R_i - C^0_i$. The tables give $\sum_i w_i EV_i / \sum_i v_i$ per
person and $100 \sum_i w_i EV_i / \sum_i w_i C^0_i$.

\textbf{Decomposition (from version 0.8.1).} Consumption per person in the
simulation is $r^1_i = r^0_i\,f_i/P_i$, where the nominal factor $f_i$ is the
product of the changes in the household's income components and in its
group's direct tax and savings rates. For a subset $S$ of the $K$ channels,
$r^S_i$ takes the channels in $S$ at their simulated values and the others at
their base values; $r^\emptyset = r^0$ and $r^{\{1, \dots, K\}} = r^1$. The
contribution of channel $j$ to a measure $I$ is its Shapley value
\citep{shorrocks2013},
\begin{equation*}
\phi_j = \sum_{S \not\ni j} \frac{|S|!\,(K - |S| - 1)!}{K!}\,\bigl[I(r^{S \cup \{j\}}) - I(r^S)\bigr],
\qquad \sum_j \phi_j = I(r^1) - I(r^0),
\end{equation*}
the change that channel $j$ makes, averaged over the orders in which the
channels change. A channel that changes nothing contributes nothing. With
$P(\mu, L)$ a poverty measure of the distribution with mean $\mu$ and Lorenz
curve $L$, the growth and redistribution components \citep{datt1992}, in
Shapley form, are
\begin{gather*}
G = \tfrac12\bigl[P(\mu^1, L^0) - P(\mu^0, L^0) + P(\mu^1, L^1) - P(\mu^0, L^1)\bigr],\\
R = \tfrac12\bigl[P(\mu^0, L^1) - P(\mu^0, L^0) + P(\mu^1, L^1) - P(\mu^1, L^0)\bigr],
\end{gather*}
with $G + R = P(\mu^1, L^1) - P(\mu^0, L^0)$, computed from the distributions
$r^0 \mu^1/\mu^0$ and $r^1 \mu^0/\mu^1$.

\textbf{Fiscal incidence (from version 0.8.2).} Following the Commitment to
Equity method \citep{lustig2018}, with market income $y^m_i$, direct taxes
$D_i$, direct transfers $B_i$, indirect taxes $I_i$, indirect subsidies $S_i$
and in-kind benefits $K_i$, the income concepts are
\begin{equation*}
y^n = y^m - D, \quad y^d = y^n + B, \quad y^c = y^d - I + S, \quad y^f = y^c + K,
\end{equation*}
net market, disposable, consumable and final income, measured per person
over persons. Persons are ranked by market income per person; with $F_i$ the
mid-point of person $i$'s cumulative share of the weights in that order
(tied values sharing their mid-point), the concentration coefficient of $t$ is
\begin{equation*}
C_t = \frac{2 \sum_i v_i\, t_i\, (F_i - 1/2)}{\sum_i v_i\, t_i},
\end{equation*}
the Gini coefficient when $t$ is market income itself. The Kakwani index
\citep{kakwani1977} is $C_T - G_m$ for a tax and $G_m - C_T$ for a benefit;
the redistributive effect is $G_m - G_f$, the Reynolds--Smolensky index
\citep{reynolds1977} $G_m - C_f$, and reranking $G_f - C_f$. The marginal
contribution of an instrument to a measure is the measure of final income
without it less the measure with it. Fiscal impoverishment
\citep{higgins2016} is the share of persons with $y^c_i < y^m_i$ who are poor
in consumable income. In the simulation, market income changes as the
household's income components, or as its group's income $\V{YI}_h$; direct
taxes as market income times the group's direct tax rate $\V{TINS}_h$;
transfers as the group's transfers from the government; indirect taxes as
nominal spending times the household's tax wedge, $\sum_j s_{ij} w_j$ with
$w_j = 1 - (1 - tq_j\,\V{TQADJ})/(1 + tv_j\,\V{TVADJ})$ the share of sales
tax and VAT in the price of commodity $j$, at the shares of the household's
budget at the new prices; subsidies as nominal spending; and in-kind benefits
as the model's spending on their target, $\V{PQ}_c\,\V{QG}_c$ for a commodity
or the health insurance fund's purchases $\sum_c \V{PQ}_c\,\V{QSHI}_c$. All
are then deflated by the household's price index.

\textbf{Reweighting (from version 0.8.3).} With the household
characteristics $x_{ik} \ge 0$ and targets $T_k = \rho_k \sum_i w_i x_{ik}$,
where $\rho_k$ is the model's ratio of employment, $\sum_a \V{QF}_{fa} /
\sum_a \V{QF}^0_{fa}$ for factor $f$ or $\V{QF}_{fa}/\V{QF}^0_{fa}$ for factor
$f$ in activity $a$, or a ratio given, the weights of the simulation solve
\begin{equation*}
\min_{w^1} \sum_i w^1_i \ln\frac{w^1_i}{w_i} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_i w^1_i x_{ik} = T_k,
\end{equation*}
so that $w^1_i = w_i \exp(x_i'\lambda)$, with $\lambda$ found by Newton's
method until the largest relative deviation from the targets is below
$10^{-12}$ \citep{wittenberg2010}. This is the reweighting step of the
macro-micro approach of \citet{corral2022}, with the targets taken from the
same model as the price and income changes.

\textbf{Standard errors (from version 0.8.3).} A measure
$\theta = \sum_i w_i d_i y_i / \sum_i w_i d_i n_i$, over the households of a
domain ($d_i = 1$), with $y_i = n_i r_i$ for the mean and
$y_i = n_i \mathbf{1}[r_i < z_i]((z_i - r_i)/z_i)^\alpha$ for FGT$_\alpha$, has
the linearized values $u_i = w_i d_i (y_i - \theta n_i) / \sum_j w_j d_j n_j$
and the variance
\begin{equation*}
\widehat V(\theta) = \sum_h \frac{n_h}{n_h - 1} \sum_{j \in h} (u_{hj} - \bar u_h)^2,
\end{equation*}
where $u_{hj}$ is the sum of $u_i$ over cluster $j$ of stratum $h$, $n_h$ the
number of clusters in the stratum, and $\bar u_h$ their mean: the variance of
\cmd{svy: ratio}. The change uses $u^1_i - u^0_i$, with the simulation's
weights in $u^1$. A stratum with a single cluster makes the variance
missing.

\textbf{Bands (from version 0.8.3).} For each draw $d$ kept by
\manref{cge_sensitivity}, every measure for all households is computed as for
a simulation; the bands are the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of the draws,
by the definition of \cmd{_pctile}, and their standard deviation.
